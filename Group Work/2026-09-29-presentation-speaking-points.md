### Slide 4 — QTPP: CS1 targets and CS2 implications

**ICH alignment:** **Q8(R2)** — product targets and CQA links; **Q9(R1)** — preliminary priorities.

- **QTPP — Quality Target Product Profile:** The quality characteristics we want the finished medicine to have. Here: a 20 mg immediate-release oral atorvastatin tablet with a proposed 24-month shelf-life. **QTPP sets the targets; CQAs are the measurable quality attributes needed to achieve them.**

- **Form / route:** “Atorvastatin already works as a once-daily oral treatment. A tablet is convenient for patients to take themselves, without infusion equipment.”
- **Strength:** “The 20 mg means the amount of atorvastatin, not the whole tablet’s weight. Getting the tablet weight right does not necessarily mean we have the correct drug content.”
- **Clinical performance:** “Using the same drug and strength as the reference does not automatically establish bioequivalence. How we formulate and manufacture the tablet can affect its performance.”
- **Dose consistency:** “The batch may contain the correct total amount of drug, but it could be unevenly distributed between tablets. That is why uniform mixing and consistent die filling matter.”
- **Release:** “Smaller drug particles generally provide more surface area for dissolution. Stronger compression can make it harder for liquid to enter the tablet. We need to investigate how these factors affect our formulation.”
- **Purity:** “Even if the incoming API meets its impurity limits, degradation can occur during manufacture or storage. Checking the raw material alone is therefore insufficient.”
- **Stability / packaging:** “Passing the tests today does not tell us whether the tablets will still pass after two years. The packaging must protect them, and stability studies must confirm that it does.”


### Slide 5 — CQAs: why these priorities?

**ICH alignment:** **Q8(R2)** — identifying CQAs; **Q9(R1)** — explaining their priorities.

*Preliminary CS2 priorities for investigation and control; all CQAs still need appropriate controls.*

- **Identity — High:** Wrong API defeats treatment and may harm patients; identity is a fundamental safeguard.
- **Related substances / degradation products — High:** Potential safety effects; compatibility and degradation behaviour remain uncertain.
- **Dissolution — High:** Particle size, excipients and compression may affect release—a main focus of CS2.
- **Uniformity of dosage units — Medium:** Assumes effective mixing and feeding controls; their performance still needs confirmation.
- **Assay — Medium:** Ingredient control and established testing offer a straightforward approach; less unresolved development work is assumed.
- **Water content — Medium:** Impact depends on formulation sensitivity; reassess after moisture and stability studies.
- **Microbial limits — Low / monitor:** Dry tablets offer less opportunity for growth under suitable conditions; retain controls and monitoring.

### Slide 6 — Raw Material Risk Assessment

**ICH alignment:** **Q9(R1)** — FMEA; **Q8(R2)** — material-to-CQA links; **Q11** — supporting API-development context.

*Working qualitative explanation: S = severity; O = likelihood of occurrence; D = detection difficulty. Low D means easier detection. These descriptions assume the controls stated below are in place.*

- **API particle-size distribution — High:** **S high:** impaired dissolution may affect performance. **O medium:** possible lot variation. **D medium:** ordinary identity/purity tests miss size differences.
- **API solid-state / polymorphic form — High:** **S high:** form changes may affect dissolution/stability. **O medium:** possible manufacture/storage variation. **D low:** assume reliable form-specific testing.
- **API purity / related substances — Medium:** **S high:** unacceptable impurities may harm patients. **O low:** assume effective supplier/process controls. **D low:** assume suitable impurity testing.
- **API / excipient water content — High:** **S high:** moisture may impair stability/performance. **O medium:** variable moisture exposure. **D medium:** sampled checks may miss later uptake.
- **Disintegrant grade / level — Medium:** **S high:** poor breakup may impair dissolution. **O low:** assume fixed grade and controlled quantity. **D medium:** identity/quantity checks do not establish performance.
- **Lubricant level / mixing exposure — High:** **S high:** excessive lubrication may impair release/strength. **O medium:** mixing exposure may vary. **D medium:** normal weight/appearance may conceal effects.

*All six have potentially serious consequences; assumed occurrence and detection differences explain the relative priorities.*

### Slide 8 — Design Space

**ICH alignment:** **Q8(R2)** — design-space development; **Q9(R1)** — prioritising variables for investigation.

*Draft. These are proposed ranges to investigate, not demonstrated acceptable limits.*

- **Blending time:** Too little mixing may leave uneven drug distribution; the proposed 10–20 minutes needs confirmation at the selected speed, scale and loading.
- **Drying endpoint (LOD):** Control residual moisture while avoiding unnecessary heat exposure; the proposed 1.5–3.0% needs supporting data, including separate degradation checks.
- **Compression force:** Balance tablet strength and drug release; study 8–12 kN together with press speed and relevant material variation.
- **Coating weight gain:** Control coating amount and consistency; 2–4% weight gain alone does not demonstrate moisture protection.
- **Packaging barrier:** Demonstrate sufficient protection throughout shelf-life; define the moisture-protection target and confirm performance with the actual package.
- **Decision rule:** Accept combinations only when all relevant quality criteria are met; individual ranges alone do not establish a design space.


### Slide 9 — Control Strategy Overview

**ICH alignment:** **Q8(R2)** — combined controls; **Q9(R1)** — control priorities; **Q10** — maintaining controls through the lifecycle.

*Draft. Build quality through controls at each stage, then verify the finished product.*

- **Raw materials:** The correct chemical can still have unsuitable particle size or form; check both identity/purity and properties affecting performance.
- **Blending / granulation:** Control how materials are distributed and granules form; representative sampling checks whether mixing actually achieved uniformity.
- **Drying / milling:** Drying controls moisture and heat exposure; milling controls granule size. Both can affect later processing and drug release.
- **Compression:** Weight checks help detect filling problems; hardness, thickness and friability track tablet consistency and strength. They do not replace dissolution or dose-uniformity testing.
- **Coating / packaging:** Coating checks assess coverage and appearance; qualified packaging protects against moisture during storage. Verify protection through stability studies.
- **Release testing:** Confirm the finished batch meets specifications; testing complements the earlier controls and cannot repair a poorly made batch.
- **Control principle:** Use several layers of control: suitable materials, monitored process parameters, in-process checks and final tests. Each addresses a different opportunity for failure.

### Slide 6 — earlier numerical questions (background only)

**Why is particle-size severity 5 rather than 4?**

The current material supports a high-severity concern but does not establish that 5 is more appropriate than 4. Severity rates the consequence of a specified failure, not the importance of the material itself. A possible scenario is a substantial particle-size shift causing sufficiently impaired dissolution to compromise product performance. Whether that is S5 or S4 depends on the group's severity definitions and supporting product knowledge. A particle-size change does not automatically establish a clinically important exposure change.

**Short answer for questions:** “We treated a substantial dissolution failure as a high-severity scenario. The exact distinction between 5 and 4 is provisional until we define the severity scale consistently. Even at S4, this row gives 4 × 3 × 3 = 36, which remains High under our thresholds.”

This sensitivity check supports the priority for investigation; it does not validate either severity score.

**Why not give disintegrant or lubricant failures the same severity?**

If they lead to the same magnitude of dissolution failure and the same patient consequence, they should receive the same severity score under a common scale. API-related failures are not automatically more severe than excipient-related failures. S4 instead of S5 would require an explicitly less serious consequence, supported by the defined scenario; the present table does not supply one. Easier control, lower likelihood or better detection cannot by themselves justify a lower severity score for the same consequence.

Avoid claiming that longer mixing automatically corrects average strength.
- Compression can also affect dosage-unit uniformity through feeding and die filling, even though this slide lists only dissolution and tablet integrity.
- [ICH Q8(R2), Part I §§2.3–2.4 and Annex §§2.3–2.5](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/Q8_R2_Guideline.pdf) supports process-development studies, justification of packaging protection, and evidence-based selection of design-space variables. It does not prescribe these scores or study decisions.


### Slide 7 — coating detection question

**Why is D Medium if appearance cannot confirm moisture protection?**

D rates how reliably existing controls detect the defined failure before release, not how many tests are needed or whether testing is visual. A dedicated test can support good detection if it is suitable, sensitive, representative and performed at the right time. Merely needing an extra procedure does not make D High.

For this table, Medium could be a working assumption if existing appearance, weight-gain and coating-consistency checks detect some coating problems but leave gaps for hidden functional defects. These checks do not by themselves demonstrate moisture-barrier performance. If relevant functional failure has no effective detection control, Medium is not established and a higher detection-difficulty rating may be appropriate. Proposed future testing cannot be credited as an existing control.

**Short answer for questions:** “Detection difficulty depends on whether our existing controls reliably find the failure, not whether an extra test is needed. Medium assumes partial detection with some gaps. If moisture-barrier failure cannot be detected by our current controls, we would need to reassess D upward.”

**Two failure modes to distinguish:** Visible coating defects and inadequate moisture protection can have different detection ratings. The current single row combines them; the table alone does not justify one D score for both. Coating's low overall priority also depends on its actual function in this product.

**Reference:** The [AHRQ FMEA scoring table](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK43662/table/advances-davis_60.t1/?report=objectonly) bases detectability on the ability of controls to detect potential causes or failure modes. It does not assign D Medium to our coating operation.

### Slide 8 — supporting notes and questions

- **Status of the numbers:** No product-specific experimental basis for these ranges has been supplied. The September 22 preparation explicitly left numerical settings and acceptance criteria to development studies. Preserve these values as the group's proposals; do not claim they are validated, regulator-prescribed, optimal or already demonstrated acceptable.
- **Why these exact limits?** “These are proposed starting ranges for development. We would confirm or revise their endpoints through studies showing acceptable product quality across relevant combinations and material variability.” Do not invent a reason that 10 minutes is acceptable but 9 is not, or that 12 kN is acceptable but 13 is not.
- **Design space:** Independent acceptable ranges are not sufficient. Study relevant relationships and interactions, then confirm that the proposed combinations consistently meet quality criteria. Do not imply every combination inside the listed ranges works. [ICH Q8(R2), Annex §§2.4.1–2.4.5](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/Q8_R2_Guideline.pdf).
- **Blending:** Mixing time depends on equipment, speed, loading, scale and material properties. Uniformity is the direct concern; simple mixing does not necessarily change true average batch API content. Distinguish total blend time from lubricant mixing exposure, which may require its own limits.
- **Drying:** LOD measures mass lost under specified test conditions, potentially including volatiles other than water. Its suitability as a moisture endpoint needs justification; it does not measure degradation impurities. The 1.5–3.0% range therefore cannot by itself establish stability. [ICH Q6A, §3.3.2.1(e)](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/Q6A%20Guideline.pdf). Applicability of this drying step remains conditional on the manufacturing route.
- **Compression:** Force depends on tooling and equipment; a force range alone does not describe the complete compression conditions. Press speed and material variability can change its effect. The existing plan proposed a joint force–speed study.
- **Coating:** Weight gain is a measure of applied coating amount, not direct proof of barrier function or uniform coverage. Confirm what the percentage is relative to, normally the starting tablet-core mass, before calculating it. A conventional immediate-release coat should not be credited with demonstrated moisture protection without evidence.
- **Packaging:** “Meets moisture-protection target” is an unresolved performance requirement rather than a numerical range. Define a measurable target, confirm package integrity and protective performance, and support the shelf-life claim with stability data. Barrier selection and qualification alone do not constitute a process design space.
- **CPP / CMA terminology:** CPP means critical process parameter; CMA means critical material attribute. Blending time and compression force are process parameters; drying endpoint and coating weight gain are intermediate attributes/endpoints, while packaging barrier is a packaging performance property. Criticality and the header's categories remain proposals, not confirmed findings.
- **Decision rule:** A demonstrated design space should assure the relevant product-quality criteria. The four outcomes listed on the slide do not waive the other CQAs or tablet-integrity requirements. For delivery, qualify the statement as the aim of the proposed study until evidence establishes the design space.

### Slide 9 — supporting notes and questions

- The central explanation is prevention/control throughout manufacture plus verification at release and during storage. These are proposed controls; specific settings, sampling plans, acceptance criteria and demonstrated effectiveness remain to be established.
- **Water-content omission:** Slide 5 retained seven potential CQAs, including water content. The release list names six and omits water content while claiming “All final CQAs.” Suggested correction: include water content where appropriate in the justified specification/testing strategy, or explain a scientifically justified alternative. Do not claim the six listed tests directly cover all seven. Earlier-stage LOD testing does not automatically establish finished-tablet water content after later processing and storage exposure.
- **Uniformity:** Representative sampling is necessary; one convenient sample does not establish whole-blend uniformity. Blend uniformity also does not guarantee absence of segregation during subsequent transfer or feeding. Weight checks do not establish content uniformity when API concentration varies.
- **Compression:** Strength and dimensional measurements are useful process indicators, but acceptable values do not by themselves establish acceptable dissolution. Press speed and feeding controls from the earlier risk assessment remain relevant despite their omission from this overview.
- **Coating / packaging:** Weight gain and appearance cannot independently prove moisture-barrier performance. Packaging qualification, package integrity and stability data are needed to support the proposed protective function and shelf-life. Release results only describe the tested batch at release; stability monitoring assesses later change.
- **Scope:** Granulation, subsequent drying and milling remain conditional on the selected manufacturing route. “All final CQAs” is the group's supplied wording; the seven-CQA list has previously been described as potential pending development.
- [ICH Q8(R2), Part I §2 and Annex §2.5](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/Q8_R2_Guideline.pdf) describes quality built into the product and a control strategy spanning materials, manufacturing, packaging and finished-product controls. [ICH Q6A, §§2.3 and 3.3.2.1(e)](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/Q6A%20Guideline.pdf) addresses justified in-process testing and water-content testing where appropriate.
