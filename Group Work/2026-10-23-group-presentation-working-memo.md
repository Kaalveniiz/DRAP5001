### Slide 4 — QMS Processes (Lifecycle)

This slide shows how our quality management system supports the controls we proposed in CS2 for atorvastatin tablets.

For a planned change, such as switching the grade of magnesium stearate, we assess whether it could affect dissolution and decide how to check the outcome. Supplier management looks at a related risk. If we change the supplier of the active ingredient, we need to assess properties such as particle size.

Take compression force as an example of process monitoring. CS2 proposed a range of 8 to 12 kilonewtons. If the force moves outside that range, we open a deviation and assess which batches could be affected and what that means for tablet quality. If the investigation identifies a cause, we take corrective action and use later production data to check whether the action worked.

Internal audit checks whether those decisions can be traced through the records. Management review looks across batches and decides whether recurring problems call for more resources, such as additional equipment maintenance.

### Slide 5 — Quality Manual Excerpt

Our quality manual explains how we would manage a planned change. Suppose we want to switch the grade of magnesium stearate in our atorvastatin tablets. The ingredient has the same name, but we still need to understand whether the new grade could affect manufacturing or tablet quality.

Development, Manufacturing and Quality would decide what comparisons the change needs. If dissolution could be affected, we would agree in advance what to measure and what result we would accept. Regulatory Affairs would check whether a filing or regulatory approval was required.

The change file should show who authorised the change and when, including the required quality-unit approval. Someone trained to prepare the request may have a different role from the person authorised to approve it. Before staff carry out the revised work, they need the updated instructions and training. After implementation, we would compare dissolution and any other relevant quality results with the criteria in the original plan and document any follow-up.

### Slide 6 — Audit Checklist – Introduction

**What does the audit cover?**

Our checklist covers selected controls for 20 milligram immediate-release atorvastatin tablets. The twenty questions follow the product from incoming materials through packaging and batch-release review. They also cover the stability programme for the packaged tablets.

**Which criteria and evidence would we use?**

We would assess the site against US drug manufacturing requirements in Parts 210 and 211 of Title 21 and its approved procedures. ICH Q10 guides our review of the quality system, while Q9 helps us decide which risks deserve attention. For evidence, we can read a record, watch the activity and ask the operator to explain it. The examples on these slides show what we would look for at our hypothetical site.

**How would we classify a finding?**

If we found a deficiency, we would document the requirement, the evidence and the reason for its grade. The Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme, or PIC/S, helps inspectors grade manufacturing deficiencies as Critical, Major or Other according to the risk involved. We adopted the PIC/S categories for this audit. A significant risk of patient harm could support a Critical finding. A serious failure of a manufacturing control could be Major. Other covers departures outside those grades. If a question does not apply, we record why. If we haven’t examined it, it stays unrated.

### Slide 7 — Audit Checklist (1/4) — Facilities & Equipment

**1 — Layout & zoning: what would support compliance?**

On a walkthrough, we could start where the tablet-press parts are stored. We’d compare the storage record with what we see. Can the operator tell which parts are clean and ready to fit, and can used parts be moved for cleaning without mixing them up? We’d follow the route used to move those parts and check whether clean parts remain protected on the way to assembly.

**2 — Environmental controls: what would support compliance?**

In the dispensing area, we’d compare temperature and humidity records with the approved limits. At the press, we’d look at the extraction maintenance records and observe whether dust is controlled while compression is running. If the humidity record shows an excursion, we’d look for a documented assessment covering the period when the area was outside its limits.

**3 — Equipment & utility qualification: what would support compliance?**

For the press itself, the qualification protocol tells us the operating range it was meant to handle. The approved report shows how it performed in that range. We’d match the equipment identifier and configuration in the report to the installed press and check how the team resolved any qualification deviations.

**4 — Maintenance & calibration: what would support compliance?**

The service log then tells us whether maintenance was kept up. For a dispensing balance, we’d match the identifier on its calibration label to the actual calibration result. If a balance was out of tolerance, we’d look for the assessment of earlier measurements made with it.

**5 — Equipment cleaning: what would support compliance?**

Before the press is used again, we’d compare its cleaning log with the approved procedure, look for the documented pre-use check and inspect the equipment. Each record has to refer to the press we’re examining. For example, the log should identify the press and record the cleaning steps completed. If the procedure requires a residue check, we’d also look for that result.

### Slide 8 — Audit Checklist (2/4) — Materials & Manufacturing Controls

**6 — Incoming material release: what would support compliance?**

We’d start with one lot of the active ingredient, atorvastatin. We’d match the lot number on the receipt and identity-test result to the quality unit’s release approval and the dispensing record. Comparing the dates tells us whether release came before use. We’d also read the receipt checks for container damage or signs of contamination and follow up anything recorded against that lot.

**7 — Material storage & status: what would support compliance?**

For a magnesium stearate lot in storage, we’d check that its container label agrees with the inventory record and that monitored conditions meet the requirements for that material. If the material spent time outside its specified storage conditions, we’d look for the decision on whether that particular lot could still be used.

**8 — Process validation: what would support compliance?**

Across batches, the validation protocol tells us what consistent performance should look like. We’d compare its criteria with the tablet-uniformity and dissolution results in the validation report, then look at routine trends to see whether that performance continued. The report should show the sampling plan and the results behind its conclusion. That lets us judge the performance of the whole process across batches.

**9 — Manufacturing execution & in-process controls: what would support compliance?**

For an individual batch, the dispensing and blending records show how staff followed the instructions. Tablet-weight checks during compression show whether an excursion occurred and which tablets needed assessment. For a weight excursion, we’d compare the sampling times with the production record to see how the team identified the potentially affected tablets.

**10 — Packaging & labelling controls: what would support compliance?**

At packaging, we’d check that the line was cleared of materials from the previous batch and that the team selected labels for the 20 milligram strength. The label records show how many were issued, used and returned, so a discrepancy can be investigated. The completed line-clearance record should identify the line and show that staff checked it before packaging began.

### Slide 9 — Audit Checklist (3/4) — Documentation & Personnel

**11 — Change control: what would support compliance?**

We can follow the magnesium stearate change through several records. The change file should explain why the team chose its comparisons. The approval and implementation dates tell us whether the right people approved the change before it took effect. The outcome review tells us whether the results met the agreed criteria. If dissolution was part of the plan, we’d compare the actual result with the criterion the team set before the change.

**12 — Document control: what would support compliance?**

The document register identifies the approved procedure and version. We’d compare the version in the register with the copy the operator can retrieve at the press. An obsolete copy still available for routine use could lead someone to follow the wrong instructions.

**13 — Personnel qualification & training: what would support compliance?**

For a tablet-press operator, we’d check training against the version that applied when the work was done. A practical assessment can show whether the operator could set up the press and respond to an excursion. If someone signed a change approval, we’d also check the record assigning that authority. The training record tells us which procedure they learned.

**14 — Personnel hygiene & gowning: what would support compliance?**

For hygiene, we’d observe hand hygiene and clothing in the dispensing area and ask when staff should change garments or report illness. Their answers should agree with the site procedure and with what we observe during dispensing.

**15 — Data integrity (ALCOA+): what would support compliance?**

For data integrity, take a dissolution result cited in the outcome review. We’d trace it back to the original instrument data, identify the analyst and test time, and use the electronic audit trail to examine any changes. The batch record provides a different link. It identifies the lots of active ingredient and excipients that went into the tablets tested. For any change to a result, we’d check who made it, when and why. We should be able to follow the reported calculation back to the retained data.

### Slide 10 — Audit Checklist (4/4) — QC & Quality Follow-up

**16 — Finished-product testing: what would support compliance?**

For a finished batch, we’d put the laboratory results beside the approved tablet specification. We’d check the required tests, such as assay, content uniformity and dissolution, and look for evidence that the methods were suitable. For dissolution, we’d match the method used for that batch to the method covered by the laboratory’s validation or verification records.

**17 — Deviation & OOS investigation: what would support compliance?**

If dissolution fell outside specification, we’d follow the original result into the investigation report. Even if a retest passed, the investigation would still need to explain the original result and assess which batches could be affected. We’d look for the scientific basis for any retesting and the evidence behind the decision on the affected product.

**18 — Batch release: what would support compliance?**

The quality unit uses the production and laboratory records to decide whether a batch can be released. We’d match its dated approval to the shipment record for the same batch to check that approval came first. If the investigation raised a concern relevant to release, the batch review should show how the team resolved it before approval.

**19 — Stability programme: what would support compliance?**

For stability, the protocol tells us when packaged tablets should be tested and under what conditions. We’d compare it with chamber logs and dated dissolution results to see whether the data continue to support shelf life. For example, if a chamber went outside its specified conditions, we’d look for an assessment of what that meant for the samples stored there.

**20 — CAPA effectiveness: what would support compliance?**

For corrective action, consider the feeder fault. The repair record shows the work was done. The later maintenance records and effectiveness review should show whether the revised maintenance programme prevented the problem from recurring, judged against the criteria in the original plan. We’d check whether staff carried out the revised inspections and what they found at subsequent visits to the feeder.

### 2. Interpretation and case boundaries

**QMS and manual:** The seven processes on Slide 4 are a practical selection. Their presentation order follows development, manufacturing and oversight, while the processes apply across lifecycle stages. A quality-manual excerpt is one selected part of the wider manual described in Q10 §1.8. The Slide 5 excerpt addresses change management; departmental roles and implementation arrangements are proposed for the hypothetical company.

**Personnel terminology:** Use “qualified and authorised personnel” for this US GMP case. Qualification and SOP training support task competence; documented responsibilities establish approval authority. Reserve “Qualified Person (QP)” for the specific EU batch-certification role, rather than using it as a title for any SOP-trained employee. The EU role is background terminology only and is not an audit criterion in this presentation; see [EMA pre-authorisation guidance, §3.3.2](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/marketing-authorisation/pre-authorisation-guidance).

**Examples:** The magnesium stearate grade change illustrates planned change assessment; the compression-force excursion illustrates investigation and batch disposition; the confirmed feeder fault and maintenance gap illustrate cause-based CAPA. Detailed walkthroughs are retained in §6 below; the speaking cues contain the short delivery version. The earlier presentation proposed a design-space study but established no demonstrated design space. A material-grade change requires formulation-specific evidence and assessment of regulatory implications. Supplier qualification alone does not establish product performance, a trend alone does not establish cause, and tablet weight alone does not establish content uniformity.

**Audit framework:** Parts 210 and 211 provide the selected US GMP benchmark, supported by Q10, Q9(R1) and approved site procedures. The checklist is a focused teaching exercise covering selected controls from incoming materials through packaging and batch-release review, plus the ongoing stability programme. It does not establish market authorisation or comprehensive site compliance. No actual company procedures, audit evidence or test results have been supplied. The hypothetical examples describe evidence to seek; document availability alone cannot demonstrate compliance.

**Classification:** Use PIC/S PI 040-1 for Critical, Major and Other deficiencies, as cited on Slide 6. C and N/A are separate checklist outcomes; N/A needs an applicability rationale and unassessed items remain unrated. This is the company's adopted internal-audit method, not a grading scheme prescribed by Part 211. Use justified site/product criteria for limits, frequencies and deadlines, including storage conditions, calibration, training, stability and investigation closure.

Item 10 checks packaging/labelling before release. #### Slide 4 — QMS Processes (Lifecycle)

Our QMS supports the tablet from development and preparation through manufacturing to ongoing oversight. These processes work together throughout the lifecycle.

During development and preparation, change control helps us evaluate proposed changes, such as switching magnesium stearate grade. Supplier management helps establish and maintain reliable sources of materials such as croscarmellose.

The magnesium stearate example continues into the audit checklist. We would investigate whether a proposed grade change could affect this formulation and justify the comparisons needed, including dissolution where relevant. We are not claiming that a grade change has been shown to alter dissolution in this formulation.

During manufacture and storage, monitoring shows how the process and product perform over time. We can track tablet-weight variation and dissolution trends. If a departure occurs, deviation management investigates the event and determines what happens to the affected tablets.

CAPA then addresses identified causes where action is needed. For example, a confirmed feeder fault and maintenance gap could lead to equipment repair and a revised maintenance programme, followed by checks that the actions worked.

Finally, internal audits check whether these processes are followed and effective. Management reviews the findings and quality trends, sets priorities, and provides resources for improvement.

#### Slide 5 — Quality Manual Excerpt

**Expanded manual wording — reference only:** The full wording below is retained for preparation; use the compact four-row table in the slide-content section for the deck.

| Section | Manual excerpt |
|:--------|:---------------|
| Purpose and scope | A documented change management system supports continual improvement while **protecting product quality**. It applies to **planned changes** to materials, manufacturing processes, equipment, analytical methods, and packaging for atorvastatin tablets. |
| Assessment and responsibilities | Relevant experts from Development, Manufacturing, Quality, and Regulatory Affairs assess proposed changes. The assessment considers **scientific justification, potential quality risks, and regulatory filing requirements**. The extent of evaluation is **proportionate to the risk and product lifecycle stage**. |
| Approval and implementation | Changes require documented review and approval by **qualified and authorised personnel before implementation**, including required **quality-unit approval**. Affected documents are updated, and personnel complete relevant SOP training before performing revised activities. |
| Review of outcomes | **Evaluation criteria are defined before implementation**. Afterwards, the company reviews the results to confirm that the change **achieved its objectives without adversely affecting product quality**. Any unresolved concerns receive documented follow-up. |

**Detailed explanation:**

The previous slide introduced our quality processes. This slide shows how we would describe change management in our company's quality manual. We can explain its four parts using the proposed magnesium stearate grade change.

First, purpose and scope. Suppose we want to use a different grade of magnesium stearate in our atorvastatin tablets. This is a planned material change, so it enters our change management system.

Second, assessment and responsibilities. Even though the ingredient has the same name, we need to assess whether the new grade could affect manufacturing or tablet quality, including dissolution. Development, Manufacturing, Quality, and Regulatory Affairs contribute their expertise. We decide what comparisons or studies are needed and set the evaluation criteria before making the change.

Third, approval and implementation. Qualified and authorised personnel review and approve the proposed change before implementation. Under our US GMP framework, the quality unit reviews and approves production and process-control procedures, including changes, and procedures or specifications affecting product quality. Regulatory Affairs determines the applicable submission or regulatory approval requirements. We meet those requirements as applicable, update affected documents, and ensure relevant personnel complete SOP training before performing the revised activities.

Qualification, training and authority have different roles. Someone may be trained and competent to prepare the magnesium stearate change request, while approval belongs to designated quality-unit personnel. Completing the change-control SOP training does not itself confer approval authority; the person's assigned responsibilities must also establish that authority.

Finally, review of outcomes. We examine the agreed results, including dissolution data where relevant, to check that the change achieved its purpose without adversely affecting quality. If the results raise concerns, we investigate and document the follow-up. This is a hypothetical example; we are describing how we would manage the change, rather than claiming that the new grade has already been tested.

This item follows manufacture because it examines how the tablets are packaged and identified before release. Correct tablet test results alone cannot establish that the correct labels and packaging were used. Sources: 21 CFR §§211.125 and 211.130, mapped in the preparation notes.

#### Slide 9 — Audit Checklist (3/4) — Documentation & Personnel

These five items follow preparation through execution: assess planned changes, control the resulting instructions, qualify and train personnel, follow hygiene and gowning requirements, and create reliable, traceable records. These systems support the manufacturing activities on Slide 8; they do not begin only after packaging. Change control also includes later outcome review, and data integrity applies from the moment data are generated. The following examples describe what would support a compliant finding; they are not actual audit results.

**11 — Change control: what would support compliance?**

A sampled change file records the proposal, quality-risk and regulatory assessments, approvals before implementation, and the subsequent outcome review. For the magnesium stearate grade-change example, evidence would show the justified comparison plan, predefined criteria, required approvals, affected document/training updates, and results supporting acceptability after implementation. Regulatory submission or approval requirements are assessed for the particular change. This applies the policy on Slide 5 to an actual type of record an auditor would examine; no grade change or favourable result is claimed to have occurred.

The slide's two checks are **assessment and approval before implementation** and **review of outcomes**. The auditor asks why the proposed comparisons were suitable for this formulation and whether the results met the agreed criteria. Dissolution is included where relevant to that assessment; a grade-related effect is not assumed.

**12 — Document control: what would support compliance?**

The document register, approval history, and copies available at the point of use agree. Superseded procedures are retained as controlled historical records but cannot be mistaken for current instructions. For example, the tablet-press operator can retrieve the effective cleaning procedure and an authorised blank record, while an obsolete version has been withdrawn from routine use. This tests control of the instructions; Item 9 checks whether the manufacturing instructions were followed.

**13 — Personnel qualification & training: what would support compliance?**

Qualification and training records match the person’s assigned duties, and observation, interview, or a suitable assessment demonstrates that the person can perform the task correctly. For a tablet-press operator, acceptable evidence could include documented practical assessment of setup, in-process checks, and response to an excursion, with training on applicable procedures and ongoing GMP training. A course attendance signature alone does not demonstrate competence. Training frequency is sufficient for the duties and risks; Part 211 does not impose a universal annual refresher interval. Practical assessment is a proposed way to demonstrate capability, not a prescribed universal test format.

Check that training covers the current SOP version applicable to the assigned task, including relevant revisions before the person performs the changed activities. Qualification reflects suitable education, training and/or experience; competence means being able to perform the task correctly. Where the task includes approval, review the documented assignment of authority as well as qualification and training. For the magnesium stearate change, a trained request preparer and the authorised quality-unit approver may have different responsibilities. Training completion alone does not grant approval authority.

**14 — Personnel hygiene & gowning: what would support compliance?**

Observed practices follow the approved hygiene and gowning procedures for the production area and task. Personnel wear clean, appropriate clothing and any protective coverings needed to prevent product contamination. For a hypothetical dispensing or compression area, the auditor checks the required head/hair covering and workwear, appropriate hand-hygiene practices, and how soiled garments are changed. The specific garments and changing steps follow the justified site procedure; sterile gowning is not assumed for nonsterile tablets.

Staff interviews should demonstrate understanding of when to change protective clothing and how to report illness or exposed lesions that could affect product quality. Appropriate restrictions on product contact are implemented when needed. Acceptable evidence combines the procedure with observed practice; training attendance alone does not establish compliance. Item 13 separately assesses qualifications, training, and demonstrated task competence.

**15 — Data integrity (ALCOA+): what would support compliance?**

ALCOA+ means **Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate**, plus **Complete, Consistent, Enduring, and Available**. These principles apply to both paper and electronic records. Keep the acronym on the slide and explain the attributes here as needed.

For an illustrative dissolution test, acceptable evidence identifies the analyst and test time, preserves original instrument data or a true copy with the required context, and allows the reported calculation to be checked. All relevant results are retained; any exclusion has a scientific justification and a traceable review. Electronic access permissions and audit trails show who entered or changed data and why. Retention and retrieval checks demonstrate that records remain readable and available for the required period. Paper corrections preserve the original entry and identify the correction under the approved procedure.

Batch traceability is included in this item. Starting with a selected batch number, the auditor follows linked records for component lots and quantities, equipment, processing steps, results, deviations, and documented review. For example, the atorvastatin batch can be traced back to its API and magnesium stearate lots and tablet press, with its in-process results and review history retrievable. The linked records and their underlying data must meet ALCOA+; missing links, unexplained gaps, or selectively reported results undermine the evidence. Final batch-release authority remains for the last topic group.

**Terminology source:** [MHRA, GXP Data Integrity Guidance and Definitions, §§3.10 and 6.1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5aa2b9ede5274a3e391e37f3/MHRA_GxP_data_integrity_guide_March_edited_Final.pdf#page=5). The MHRA source supports the ALCOA+ terminology; the US GMP criteria and FDA guidance remain the main basis for the case’s data-integrity assessment.

#### Slide 10 — Audit Checklist (4/4) — QC & Quality Follow-up

These five items follow finished-product testing, investigation of relevant deviations/OOS results, the batch-release decision, ongoing stability monitoring, and CAPA effectiveness follow-up. Investigations begin whenever a problem arises, including during manufacture or stability testing. Release-critical concerns must be resolved before release; stability and CAPA monitoring can run in parallel, and longer-term CAPA follow-up requires a justified disposition and appropriate controls. They are proposed audit checks with hypothetical examples, not actual findings.

**16 — Finished-product testing: what would support compliance?**

The laboratory uses approved specifications, a suitable sampling plan, and methods supported by validation or appropriate verification. Sampled batch results cover the required tests and meet the applicable acceptance criteria before release. For the atorvastatin tablets, proposed examples include identity, assay, content uniformity, dissolution, and relevant impurities, according to the actual approved specification. The auditor checks that the method used matches the approved method and that its suitability is demonstrated. Q2(R2), Validation of Analytical Procedures, supports method validation; compendial methods require suitability verification under actual conditions of use as applicable. No numerical acceptance limit is invented for the case.

This connects with the magnesium stearate example in Item 11, but asks a different question. Item 11 examines how the change was assessed and controlled; Item 16 examines the required finished-tablet tests, their methods and results. Passing release tests alone do not demonstrate that the change assessment was adequate. The three tests named on the slide are illustrative, not a complete specification.

**17 — Deviation & OOS investigation: what would support compliance?**

An investigation records the event, assesses plausible causes using evidence, identifies potentially affected batches, and reaches a justified conclusion on product impact and disposition. A compression-force excursion is a deviation example; a confirmed result outside the dissolution specification is an OOS example. OOS means out of specification. A passing retest alone does not justify discarding the original OOS result. Retesting or resampling must be scientifically justified, and relevant manufacturing causes and other potentially affected batches must be considered. No universal 30-day closure requirement is assumed; timing and escalation follow justified site procedures and risk.

**18 — Batch release: what would support compliance?**

A documented decision by the authorised quality unit follows review of the required production and laboratory records, satisfactory test results, and an adequate assessment of deviations or OOS investigations relevant to release. The decision date and batch status agree with distribution records, demonstrating no shipment before approval. A hypothetical example is an atorvastatin batch with its quality approval recorded before the warehouse releases it for distribution. Passing laboratory results alone do not constitute batch release. Release-critical concerns must be resolved; longer-term CAPA effectiveness follow-up is managed separately where a justified disposition and appropriate controls permit it. No actual released batch is claimed.

**19 — Stability programme: what would support compliance?**

An approved protocol identifies batches, the marketed container-closure system, storage conditions, test intervals, methods, and acceptance criteria. Records demonstrate that due timepoints were tested, chamber conditions were controlled, and results were reviewed. For example, a dissolution trend for packaged atorvastatin tablets is assessed for its implications for the assigned shelf life. Missed tests or chamber excursions require documented assessment and follow-up. Stability storage conditions follow the study protocol and may differ from routine warehouse conditions. 21 CFR §211.166 is the direct US GMP criterion; Q1A(R2) supplies supporting stability principles subject to the scope qualification already recorded.

**20 — CAPA effectiveness: what would support compliance?**

The CAPA plan links actions to the investigated cause, assigns owners and due dates, and defines how effectiveness will be assessed. In the earlier feeder example, a confirmed component fault and maintenance gap could lead to repair and a revised preventive-maintenance programme. Subsequent production and maintenance evidence should demonstrate success against predefined criteria, such as the required consistency of tablet weights and completion of the revised inspections. A closed action or training attendance record alone does not establish effectiveness. Item 17 examines cause and product impact; Item 20 examines whether the resulting actions solve the problem and sustain improvement. Longer-term actions require appropriate interim controls where needed.
